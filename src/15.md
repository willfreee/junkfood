## Final rewrite

# The "just one" illusion

How many times have you told yourself "just one"? Just one chip, just one cookie, and how many times has "just one" actually been one? Almost never. The chip becomes the bag, and "just one" becomes "just the whole thing."

The voice that says "just one" isn't your rational mind. It's the parasite, asking you to open the door just a crack. You've blamed yourself for a failure that was engineered into the food. This chapter shows why "just one" can never work, and why the easiest, most satisfying amount of junk food to eat is none.

### A catalog of failures

You know the scenario. It's evening, a craving surfaces, and your mind proposes a compromise: *"Just a handful."* The first bite hits, the engineered bliss point floods your brain with dopamine, and you get a brief relief before, almost instantly, more is demanded. Your hand reaches again, the package empties, and you're left with the familiar aftermath: heaviness, guilt, a broken promise.

You've interpreted this as weakness. But the product was functioning exactly as designed. You didn't fail; the food succeeded. Its job is to make "just one" impossible.

Suppose you tried to take "just one drop" of water from a dam by poking a small hole in it. The water wouldn't politely release a single drop and stop. The pressure behind the dam quickly turns the small hole into a large one, and the large hole into a flood. The structure was never designed to release single drops. It was designed to hold back a massive volume of water under pressure, and any breach, however small, will eventually release the whole thing.

Junk food is the dam and "just one" is the small hole. The engineering (the bliss point, the vanishing caloric density, the flavor enhancers, the precise chemical ratios calibrated in laboratories) creates the pressure, and once the pressure finds an opening, it floods.

### Why piece one guarantees piece twenty

The first bite triggers the bliss point, an immediate dopamine flood, and the brain wants more, now. At the same time, vanishing caloric density bypasses your calorie-counting mechanisms: the food dissolves so fast your brain registers almost nothing. Flavor enhancers keep sensory-specific satiety from kicking in, so the hundredth bite tastes almost as intense as the first.

The combined effect is a neurological cascade that makes stopping at one nearly impossible. The first piece amplifies the craving instead of satisfying it. Stopping at one means overriding one of the most powerful engineered cascades in the modern food environment, which takes far more than moderate restraint.

"Just one" piece of junk food is like "just one" pull on a slot machine programmed to pay out on the first pull. The win triggers the brain's reward system, which immediately demands another pull to see if the streak continues. The machine was designed to create that demand, so the "choice" to keep playing isn't free. The machine is doing its job, and it's very good at it.

### Why "just one" makes everything worse

Eating a small amount doesn't satisfy the craving. It amplifies it. Scratching a mosquito bite works the same way: "just one scratch" releases more histamine and makes the itch worse. The first bite irritates the underlying chemical state, so the craving, which might have faded on its own, now has fresh fuel and burns brighter. "Just one" turns a manageable itch into an unmanageable demand, and you'd have been better off eating nothing.

Picture trying to put out a small fire by throwing a single log onto it. The log feeds the fire. A fire that was dying down now has fresh fuel and burns hotter, and it takes more effort to control than if you'd let it burn out on its own. "Just one" is the log and the craving is the fire.

"Just one" isn't a compromise between zero and the whole package. It's often worse than eating the whole package, because it combines the amplified craving of partial consumption with the guilt of having "given in" without getting the full experience. If you eat the whole package, at least the craving is temporarily silenced and the Little Monster is fed into a stupor. If you eat just one, the craving is louder than before, and you're fighting an internal battle with diminished resources. Zero is easier than one because it doesn't wake the Little Monster or start the negotiation. Zero is clean, complete, and more satisfying than "just one" could ever be.

### The voice in disguise

The "just one" thought arrives in a costume: reasonable, moderate, sounding like your own voice. *"You've been doing so well. One won't hurt."* Watch what happens after the first bite, when the reasonable tone vanishes. *"Might as well finish it. Start fresh tomorrow."* It's the same entity with a different strategy. The moderate opening is the disguise, and the compulsive follow-up is the reality.

It's like a burglar dressed as a delivery person. Once the door is open, the uniform comes off. The voice that proposes "just one" knows it won't end at one and is counting on that. When it appears, you can say simply: "No. I know where this goes."

### The closed door

A fear might be surfacing. It's the Big Monster's final defense in this arena, and it sounds like this: *"If I can never have 'just one,' I have to commit to 'none.' And 'none' sounds extreme and rigid. It sounds like the absolutism that leads to orthorexia and disordered eating and being the difficult person at parties. Surely 'none' is harder than 'just one,' and moderation, even if it's difficult, is the healthier goal."*

This fear rests on a false premise: that "none" is more extreme and more difficult than "one." The reality is the opposite. "None" is far easier.

"None" requires a single decision, made once: "I don't eat junk food." The boundary is clean. When someone offers you chips at a party, the answer is a simple, automatic "no thanks," with no negotiation, no internal argument, and no assessment of whether today has been stressful enough to "earn" an indulgence. The decision has already been made, and the door is closed.

"Just one" requires a continuous series of decisions, each made under increasing neurological pressure. Should I have another? Just one more? Where's the line between "moderate" and "too much," and am I still in control? Each decision depletes the same finite willpower reserve and is harder than the last, because the craving is amplifying while the resistance weakens. The effort of restraint consumes the whole experience, and restraint usually fails.

Which is easier: keeping a door completely closed, or holding it open exactly one inch while something on the other side pushes with all its weight? The closed door takes no effort, because the latch is engaged and the door stays shut on its own. The one-inch gap takes constant, exhausting resistance against an increasing force. Your muscles tire and your attention wavers until, eventually, the door flies open.

"None" is the closed door and "just one" is the one-inch gap. The apparent moderation of "just one" is the most difficult, most draining, least sustainable position possible: maximum effort for minimum reward, with all the mental exhaustion of trying to moderate and none of the satisfaction of freedom.

"None" is the path of least resistance, a single clean decision that eliminates the need for a thousand smaller ones. Once the desire for junk food is dismantled and the Big Monster's illusions have been seen through, "none" doesn't feel like deprivation or rigidity. It feels like peace, and the door stays closed because nothing on the other side appeals to you.

### The most satisfying amount

Here is the most counterintuitive claim in this chapter, and perhaps in the book: "none" is more satisfying than "just one," as well as easier.

"Just one" always leaves you wanting more, because the product is engineered to produce exactly that effect. The bliss point, the vanishing caloric density, and the flavor enhancers are all designed so that one piece creates a demand for the next. Eating one and stopping is an exercise in frustration. You've engaged the cycle without completing it, woken the Little Monster without putting it back to sleep, and scratched the itch only to make it worse. It's the least satisfying amount of junk food to eat, because it delivers all the craving amplification with none of the temporary relief that finishing the package would provide.

"None" leaves you free. The craving arises, and you recognize it for what it is: the Little Monster, a mild physical itch, and no genuine hunger. You don't feed it. The craving peaks and then, because it isn't reinforced with fresh chemical fuel, it passes, and you move on with your day. There's no guilt, no physical heaviness, no crash, no renewed craving spike an hour later, and no internal argument about whether to have more. You get the clean, complete satisfaction of not having engaged the cycle at all.

"Just one" is like trying to satisfy your thirst with a single drop of water. The drop doesn't quench the thirst and makes you more aware of how thirsty you are. It's the worst of both worlds: the thirst is louder, and you've broken your commitment without getting the relief of a full drink. "None" means recognizing that you're not thirsty. You're experiencing a chemically induced craving that mimics thirst, and the chemical won't help. The most satisfying response is to see through the craving entirely and let it pass.

If you eat "just one," you spend the next hour fighting an internal battle. Even if you "win" and somehow stop at one through sheer force of will, you don't feel triumphant. You feel depleted, irritable, and vaguely cheated, as if you barely survived a battle you'll have to fight again tomorrow. If you eat "none," you spend that same hour free. The craving came, the craving went, and life continued. No battle was fought because none was necessary.

The easiest, most peaceful, and most satisfying amount of junk food to eat is none. You aren't being good or following a rule. The product is designed so that any amount above zero is an exercise in frustration, and zero is the only amount that leaves you free.

The voice that says "just one" will still appear from time to time. It's been reinforced for years, and old patterns don't vanish overnight. But now you know what it is: the parasite, asking you to open the door just a crack, and once the door is open it doesn't ask permission for what comes next.

You don't have to open the door. On the other side of a closed one is something the "just one" voice never wanted you to discover: peace, quiet, freedom, and the end of the internal war.
