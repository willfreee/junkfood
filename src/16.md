## Final rewrite

# Why moderation is a myth

"Everything in moderation." It sounds like wisdom. But a question hides inside this advice: moderation for whom? A moderate amount of alcohol for an alcoholic? Or are there substances where "moderation" is just a prettier word for "ongoing struggle"?

The moderation myth is one of the Big Monster's most effective defenses. It keeps you in the most exhausting position possible: the endless middle ground where you're always managing, always negotiating, and never free. This chapter dismantles it. The aim is to show that with certain substances, complete escape is easier, more peaceful, and the only approach that works, and extremism has no part in it.

### The seductive reasonableness of moderation

You want to be the kind of person who can enjoy junk food occasionally without being controlled by it. You've seen people like this, the friend who eats three chips and walks away. You've tried to be like them, and every time the attempt has collapsed.

Those "naturally moderate" eaters aren't more disciplined than you. Their brains simply respond differently to the engineered bliss point, and they were never fully trapped in the first place. Comparing yourself to them is comparing apples to oranges.

Moderation is a fine principle for things that don't hijack your brain's reward circuitry. A moderate amount of walking is healthy, reading is enriching, and conversation with friends is connecting. These self-regulate because the body and mind have built-in mechanisms for signaling "enough."

Would you advise "moderate" heroin use, or "moderate" cigarette smoking for someone whose lungs are already damaged? You wouldn't, because you recognize intuitively that some substances, by their nature, make moderation practically impossible for the people whose brains are susceptible to them. So the question is whether moderation applies to a substance engineered in a laboratory to override your brain's natural "stop" signals.

Some people can drink alcohol occasionally without developing a dependency, and some can't. The difference comes from a complex interaction of genetics, neurochemistry, and life circumstances, and moral character has nothing to do with it. You wouldn't tell an alcoholic to "just drink moderately, like Sarah does." Sarah was never an alcoholic, and her brain responds to alcohol differently. The advice is meaningless because the starting conditions are completely different.

Junk food works the same way. Some brains are more susceptible to the bliss point hijack than others. If you're reading this book, if you've struggled with junk food for years, if "just one" has never been just one, if moderation has failed you repeatedly, yours is one of them. Telling you to "just eat in moderation" is like telling someone with a severe peanut allergy to "just eat peanuts in moderation." It misunderstands the nature of the problem.

### The difference between moderation and managed obsession

True moderation is characterized by genuine indifference. The moderate drinker doesn't spend their days thinking about alcohol, which contrasts sharply with your experience of junk food: the constant mental tally, the negotiation, the questions about when and how much. That is managed obsession, a full-time cognitive job. You've traded consuming the food for consuming yourself with thoughts about it.

Suppose someone spends hours every day thinking about alcohol: when they can have their next drink, how much they're allowed, whether yesterday was too much, whether they've been "good" this week, whether they've earned an indulgence. Even if their actual drinking stays within medically defined "moderate" limits, would you describe their relationship with alcohol as healthy? Or would you recognize that the mental preoccupation is a form of bondage, however much they drink?

The mental preoccupation is the cage, and the quantity consumed only gives its dimensions. Your "moderation" has kept you in a cage slightly larger than total abstinence, and you've called it "balance" because the door is open a crack. You're still inside, still spending your days measuring the bars.

### Why moderation is the most exhausting position

Moderation isn't the easy middle path. It's the hardest position of all. Complete indulgence takes almost no cognitive effort, and complete abstinence through genuine disinterest takes almost none, but moderation demands constant vigilance. Every encounter requires a decision, every day reignites the debate, and the willpower battery is constantly drained without being recharged.

It's like driving with one foot resting on the brake at all times. Total abstinence is taking your foot off both pedals and getting out of the car, while moderation is the endless, exhausting middle where you're always braking and never at rest.

The "extreme" option of abstinence, achieved by dismantling desire and without imposing willpower, is the path of least resistance. It's the single decision that eliminates the need for a thousand smaller ones, the closed door that doesn't need constant pushing to stay shut. Moderation is the one-inch gap from the previous chapter, held open against an increasing force, demanding everything from you and giving very little back.

### The two kinds of moderation

Rejecting moderation for junk food doesn't mean rejecting it for everything. Moderation with real food is effortless, and your body handles it automatically, like breathing. Moderation with engineered substances is a constant, exhausting performance, like trying to breathe underwater.

You don't need to learn to moderate junk food. You need to escape it entirely. Once you do, real moderation with real food emerges on its own, because your body knows how and has been doing it for millions of years.

### The peace of none

The Big Monster has probably convinced you that giving up on moderation means giving up on freedom. The opposite is true. Giving up on moderation, and embracing "none" from genuine disinterest instead of willpower, is the most liberating step in the process.

When you stop trying to moderate junk food, you stop thinking about it. The mental tally, the negotiation, the guilt about having too much, and the resentment about having too little all disappear, along with the exhausting internal debate about when and whether and how much. The mental bandwidth the moderation project consumed is suddenly free, and you get back hours of cognitive life you didn't realize you were spending.

You don't miss the junk food because you've seen through the illusions that made it appealing: the treat that wasn't a treat, the comfort that wasn't comfort, the energy borrowed against a crash, and the taste engineered to override your senses. Once those illusions are dismantled, junk food stops looking like a forbidden pleasure and looks like what it is, an industrial chemical trap that never gave you anything of value.

You don't miss the moderation struggle either, because the struggle was a form of suffering. The constant management, perpetual vigilance, and never-ending negotiation were never freedom. They were a second job you were working for free, and quitting it is a relief.

Picture two people who have stopped smoking. One still craves a cigarette every day but resists through sheer force of will. Every encounter with a smoker, every sight of a cigarette, and every moment of stress triggers an internal battle. They're abstaining, and they aren't free. The other has lost the desire for cigarettes. They walk past smokers without a second thought, and the smell of smoke triggers mild distaste and no longing. They don't need willpower because there's nothing to resist.

Both people abstain, and only one is free. This book aims at the second kind of freedom, where "none" is the natural expression of a desire that no longer exists and no rule you follow. When you no longer want junk food, not eating it takes no effort. The door stays closed because nothing on the other side interests you.

This isn't extremism or rigidity, and it isn't orthorexia or disordered eating or any of the other labels the Big Monster uses to scare you away from freedom. It's the most natural, peaceful, effortless relationship with food there is, the one you were born with before the engineering, the marketing, and the conditioning got their hooks into you. You eat real food with genuine hunger, enjoy it with functioning taste buds, and stop when the body says "enough." There are no rules, no moderation, and no struggle, just eating, satisfaction, and moving on with your life.

This is the easy path. The hard path was the one you've been on, the endless middle ground of moderation where you were always managing and never free. You don't have to walk it anymore. There's a door, it's open, and on the other side is a life where food is just food, you are just you, and the war is finally over.
