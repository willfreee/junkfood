# The instruction you won't expect

Before we go any further, I need you to promise me one thing: do not stop eating junk food. Not today, and not while you're reading this book. Keep everything exactly as it is. If you want drive-thru tonight, get it. All I ask is that you keep reading.

I know that sounds strange, a book about quitting junk food that tells you to keep eating it. Maybe you're suspicious. There's no trick here, and I won't pull the rug out later.

Notice what happened inside you when you read that instruction. If you're like most people who open a book like this, you were braced for a lecture. You were already tensing against the deprivation of all the foods you believe you love, with the familiar mix of dread and defiance that every diet and every "this time I'm serious" Monday morning has taught you to expect. Then I told you to change nothing and eat what you want. Did your shoulders drop a little? Did you feel a quiet exhale?

That feeling matters for what it shows: the fear of quitting has done far more damage to you than the junk food ever has. You've carried a panic about giving everything up, and a tug-of-war between "I should stop" and "I don't want to stop." It has worn you out, and none of it was necessary.

Think about what happens when you finish a heavy junk food meal. You know the one. The burger and fries seemed like exactly what you wanted when you ordered them, but twenty minutes later you're sitting there with a stomach full of regret, physically heavy and mentally foggy, promising yourself this was the last time. "Never again," you tell yourself. "Starting tomorrow, I'm done."

Then the word "never" lands like a stone in your chest. Your mind races through everything you're about to lose: late-night snacks, comfort food when you're stressed, treats at the movies, lazy Sunday morning pastries. The list stretches out like a prison sentence, and before you've swallowed the last bite you're already grieving.

That grief comes from what you *believe* about junk food. If those beliefs are wrong, the grief, the fear, and the struggle were all unnecessary.

This book examines those beliefs one by one, and I think you'll be surprised by what we find. That only works if you're not panicking, because you can't think clearly about a trap while you're convinced you're about to be thrown into a punishment cell. So I'm opening the cell door and telling you to walk back out into your normal life. Eat what you eat and do what you do. For now, the only thing that changes is what you understand.

### Why this book has no rules

You've been through this before: a new book, a new set of rules, then the Sunday-night binge before Monday's deprivation begins. The diet mentality creates deprivation, and deprivation creates obsession. Once you tell yourself you can't have something, it's all you can think about.

This book takes deprivation apart instead of creating it, and it puts no forbidden pedestal under junk food. We'll look at what's actually on that pedestal, up close and in full light. Once you see it clearly, you won't need rules to stay away from it. You'll simply stop wanting it.

Imagine a magician invites you on stage and says, "I'm going to perform a trick, and as I do, I'll show you exactly how it works. Watch my hands. See the mirror there. Notice the trap door under the table. I'm hiding nothing." Would watching that make you anxious, or curious? Would you feel you were missing out, or being let in on a secret?

I'm inviting you behind the curtain the same way. Keep eating whatever you're eating while I show you the wires and mirrors that made the illusion seem real. In my experience, once you've seen them clearly you can't unsee them, and the illusion stops working without any fight on your part.

### Seeing the maze from above

You might be thinking, "That sounds nice, but you don't understand how strong my cravings are." Have you ever reached the bottom of a bag of chips and barely remembered eating the second half? Your hand kept moving while your mind was elsewhere, and there was no pleasure in that second half. An automated program was running.

From inside a maze, every dead end feels like a real obstacle. From a balcony above, the correct path is plain. This book takes you up to that balcony, but you can only see the mechanism clearly while it's still running, which is why I told you to keep eating normally. I want you to see the trap for yourself.

### A question to carry with you

Here's a question to plant in your mind. You don't need to answer it today. Let it sit in the background while you keep eating what you normally eat and keep reading: *What if this stuff has never actually given me anything real?*

That sounds absurd at first. It seems to give you something: pleasure, comfort, energy, a moment of relief in a stressful day. Look closer. Replay your last drive-thru run. The anticipation peaked at the order screen, didn't it? You were deciding what to get, picturing it, already tasting it before it existed. That was the high point. When the food arrived, the first few bites were fine and more or less matched the picture in your head. By the time you were three bites past fullness (and you kept eating anyway, because that's what the food was designed to make you do), where was the pleasure or comfort?

What you actually experienced was the temporary silencing of a craving. The craving was loud and uncomfortable, eating quieted it for a while, and that quiet felt like relief. You've called it "pleasure" for so long that you've forgotten to question it.

Picture wearing shoes two sizes too small all day. Your feet are pinched, aching, and blistered until you finally take them off and feel incredible relief. Would you call that pleasure? Would you wake up the next morning thinking, "I can't wait to put those painful shoes back on so I can enjoy taking them off again"? Nobody would. You'd see the cycle for what it was: the shoes caused the pain, and taking them off ended it.

The junk food craving works the same way. The previous dose of ultra-processed food caused a blood sugar spike, the spike caused a crash, and the crash created a craving for the next dose. Eating that dose eases the craving, and the unpleasant tension goes away. You've been reading that relief as enjoyment.

"But wait," you might be thinking. "If that's true, why did the first dose feel good? I wasn't craving it before I ever tried it." Later chapters look at that thoroughly. For now, consider that most people's first experiences with ultra-processed junk food came in childhood. You were probably given a sugary cereal, a Happy Meal, a soda, or a candy bar before you were old enough to question what you were eating, so the link between those products and pleasure formed before you had the critical thinking skills to examine it. You were handed the small shoes before you knew what comfortable shoes felt like, and by the time you could question the cycle, it was already running.

What you've been calling love is the temporary relief of a manufactured tension, and this book shows you that. Once you see it clearly in your own experience, and not only in theory, the desire to eat junk food collapses on its own. You don't need willpower to resist something you no longer want.
