# The Instruction You Won't Expect

Before we go any further, I need you to make me one promise: do not stop eating junk food. Not today, not while you're reading this book. Keep everything exactly as it is. If you want drive-thru tonight, get it. All I ask is that you keep reading.

I know that sounds strange: a book about quitting junk food that tells you to keep eating it. Maybe you're suspicious. It isn't a trick, and there will be no moment where I pull the rug out.

Notice what happened inside you when you read that instruction. If you're like most people who open a book like this, you were braced for impact. You expected a lecture. You were already tensing against the imagined deprivation of all the foods you believe you love, feeling the familiar mix of dread and defiance that every diet, every health kick, every "this time I'm serious" Monday morning has taught you to expect.

Then I told you to change nothing and eat exactly what you want.

Did you feel that small release in your shoulders, that quiet exhale?

That feeling is the most important thing you'll take from this first chapter, because of what it reveals. The instant relief shows that the fear of quitting has done far more damage to you than the junk food ever has. The panic you've carried about having to give everything up, the exhausting tug-of-war between "I should stop" and "I don't want to stop," has been draining you, and it was unnecessary.

Think about what happens when you finish a heavy junk food meal. You know the one. The burger and fries seemed like exactly what you wanted when you ordered them, but twenty minutes later you're sitting there with a stomach full of regret, feeling physically heavy and mentally foggy, promising yourself that this was the last time. "Never again," you tell yourself. "Starting tomorrow, I'm done."

And then what happens? The word "never" lands like a stone in your chest. Your mind starts racing through everything you're about to lose: no more late-night snacks, comfort food when you're stressed, treats at the movies, or lazy Sunday morning pastries. The list stretches out in front of you like a prison sentence, and before you've swallowed the last bite, you're already grieving.

That grief is a reaction to what you *believe* about junk food. If those beliefs are wrong, the grief, the fear, and the struggle have all been unnecessary.

This book will examine those beliefs one by one, and I think you'll be surprised by what we find. That only works if you're not panicking. You can't think clearly about a trap while you're convinced you're about to be thrown into a punishment cell. So I'm opening the cell door and telling you to walk back out into your normal life. Eat what you eat and do what you do. For now, the only thing changing is what you understand.

### Why this book has no rules

You've been through this before. A new book, a new set of rules, and then the Sunday-night binge before Monday's deprivation begins. The diet mentality creates deprivation, and deprivation creates obsession. The moment you tell yourself you can't have something, it becomes the only thing you can think about.

This book dismantles that deprivation instead of creating it, and it puts no forbidden pedestal under junk food. We're going to examine what's actually on that pedestal, up close, in full light. Once you see it clearly, you won't need rules to stay away from it. You simply won't want it anymore.

Imagine a magician invited you on stage and said, "I'm going to perform a trick, and as I perform it, I'm going to show you exactly how it works. Watch my hands. See the mirror there. Notice the trap door under the table. I'm not hiding anything from you." Would watching that performance make you anxious, or curious? Would you feel like you were missing out, or being let in on a secret?

I'm inviting you behind the curtain in the same way. You don't have to stop watching the show, so keep eating whatever you're eating. While you do, I'll show you the wires and mirrors that made the illusion seem real. Here is what I know from experience: once you've seen them clearly, you can't unsee them, and the illusion stops working without any fight on your part.

### Seeing the maze from above

You might be thinking: *"That sounds nice, but you don't understand how strong my cravings are."* Let me ask you something. Have you ever reached the bottom of a bag of chips and barely remembered eating the second half? Your hand kept moving while your mind was elsewhere. Where was the pleasure in that second half? There wasn't any. An automated program was running.

Imagine walking through a maze from inside it. Every dead end feels like a genuine obstacle. Now imagine seeing the same maze from a balcony above, where the correct path is unmistakable. This book takes you up to that balcony. But you can only see the mechanism clearly while it's still running, which is why I told you to keep eating normally. I want you to see the trap for yourself.

### A question to carry with you

I want to plant a question in your mind. You don't need to answer it today. Let it sit in the background while you keep eating whatever you normally eat and keep reading.

Here's the question: *What if this stuff has never actually given me anything real?*

I know that sounds absurd at first. Of course it seems to give you something: pleasure, comfort, energy, a moment of relief in a stressful day, right?

Let's look closer. Think about your last drive-thru run, and really replay it in your mind. The anticipation peaked at the order screen, didn't it? You were deciding what to get, picturing it, already tasting it before it existed. That was the high point. When the food arrived, the first few bites were fine. They more or less matched the picture in your head. But by the time you were three bites past fullness (and you kept eating anyway, because that's what the food was designed to make you do), where was the pleasure or comfort?

What you actually experienced was the temporary silencing of a craving. The craving was loud and uncomfortable, and eating quieted it for a while. That quiet felt like relief, and you've called it "pleasure" for so long that you've forgotten to question it.

Imagine wearing shoes two sizes too small all day. Your feet are pinched, aching, and blistered until you finally take them off and feel incredible relief. Would you call that relief pleasure? Would you wake up the next morning thinking, "I can't wait to put those painful shoes back on so I can enjoy taking them off again"?

Of course not. That would be insane. You'd see the whole cycle for what it was: the shoes caused the pain, and taking them off relieved it. The relief wasn't a positive experience, just the end of a negative one.

The junk food craving works the same way. The previous dose of ultra-processed food created a blood sugar spike, which created a blood sugar crash, which created a craving for the next dose. Eating that dose temporarily eases the craving and the unpleasant tension goes away. You've been interpreting that relief as enjoyment.

*"But wait,"* you might be thinking. *"If that's true, then why did the first dose feel good? I wasn't craving it before I ever tried it."*

That's a fair point, and later chapters examine it thoroughly. For now, consider this: most people's first experiences with ultra-processed junk food happened in childhood. You were probably given a sugary cereal, a Happy Meal, a soda, or a candy bar before you were old enough to question what you were eating. The association between those products and pleasure was built before you had the critical thinking skills to examine it. You were handed the small shoes before you knew what comfortable shoes felt like. By the time you were old enough to question the cycle, it was already running.

This book won't teach you to resist something you love. It shows that what you've been calling love was the temporary relief of a manufactured tension. Once you see that clearly, experientially and not just intellectually, the desire to eat junk food collapses on its own. You don't need willpower to resist something you no longer want.
