# The comfort food myth

Think about the last time you ate something to feel better. You weren't hungry. You were sad, lonely, or just feeling a vague emptiness.

Did it work, or did the sadness wait patiently while you ate, then climb back into your lap the moment the food was gone?

This is the comfort food myth. The relief you feel is real: the dopamine temporarily overrides the pain. But there's a world of difference between temporarily muffling pain and healing it. Junk food has never healed a single emotion. All it has ever done is provide a brief chemical distraction from feelings that stay unchanged once the distraction ends.

### The pillow over the alarm clock

You're sad, or lonely, or bored. Your mind searches for relief and finds the solution programmed since childhood: eat junk food. As the eating begins, the sharp edge dulls and the world narrows to taste and texture, giving you a reprieve for a few minutes.

Then the food is gone and the dopamine fades, but the sadness didn't go anywhere. It was waiting, and it returns exactly as large as before, unprocessed and unhealed, after being temporarily muffled by a louder signal.

Picture pressing a pillow over a ringing alarm clock. The sound gets quieter, and you feel relief because the awful noise is no longer filling the room. But the alarm is still ringing. You haven't turned it off or addressed whatever set it off. When you lift the pillow (when the food is gone), the alarm is just as loud as before.

Junk food is the pillow and the emotion is the alarm. The relief was real because the pillow genuinely reduced the volume, and the alarm never stopped ringing. Now you've also added a new problem: your arms are tired from holding the pillow, and you're starting to realize this isn't sustainable.

The relief you feel through food isn't imaginary. The dopamine hit does temporarily override the emotional pain signal in the brain, and that is a neurochemical fact. But the relief is neurological and not emotional. The sadness has been chemically silenced, and it's right where you left it when the chemical wears off.

### The chemical loan

Sugar and fat trigger a flood of dopamine and a brief serotonin boost. For ten to twenty minutes you genuinely feel better, and that feels like comfort. It's neurochemistry, and like all drug effects it comes with a comedown.

Alcohol is a useful comparison. A drink before a party reduces social anxiety temporarily, but no one would call that a cure. The anxiety returns when the alcohol wears off, often worse. Junk food operates the same way. It's a chemical loan against future emotional pain, and what you've been calling "comfort" is a drug effect dressed in emotional language.

### The emotion that survives

The way you've been evaluating comfort eating rests on a small fraction of its total arc, so here is the full timeline.

You're lonely on a quiet evening, and the day is done, so the quiet of your home feels less like peace and more like emptiness. The feeling is uncomfortable, and your brain, following a script it learned decades ago, suggests food. It wants the specific, hyper-palatable food it knows will deliver the fastest, strongest dopamine response: ice cream, chips, delivery from the place down the street.

You eat. For the ten or fifteen minutes the eating lasts, the loneliness recedes. The dopamine is flowing, the taste is occupying your sensory attention, and the world has narrowed to something manageable. This is the phase you've been judging the whole experience on.

Then the food is gone. Even the largest portions run out, and when the eating ends, the dopamine spike begins its descent. The sensory distraction is removed, and your attention, no longer occupied by taste and texture, returns to the emotional landscape that was there all along.

The loneliness is still there, exactly where you left it and exactly as large. Nothing about it has been healed, resolved, or addressed. You spent fifteen minutes not feeling it, and now you're back to feeling it, with a set of new, unpleasant experiences on top.

Your stomach feels heavy and distended. The sugar crash is beginning, a wave of lethargy and mental fog that makes it harder to do anything that might address the loneliness, like calling a friend or going for a walk. The guilt arrives: *"I did it again even though I wasn't even hungry, so why do I keep doing this?"* Underneath the guilt sits a quiet hopelessness: *"I can't even manage my own emotions without food. What's wrong with me?"*

The loneliness was joined by physical discomfort, a metabolic crash, guilt, and self-reproach. Your emotional state after comfort eating is worse than before it, so the "comfort" was a net negative.

Think of someone grieving a loss. A friend comes over, and instead of sitting with them, listening, or offering presence and understanding, says, "Don't think about it. Here, watch this distracting video for ten minutes." The video works temporarily and the grief recedes from immediate awareness. When it ends, the grief is exactly where it was, and the grieving person feels slightly worse because they've postponed the difficult, ultimately healing process of grieving.

Junk food is the distracting video. It helps you avoid emotion and doesn't help you process it, and avoidance only prolongs. The emotion is still waiting when the distraction ends. Every time you postpone it, you teach your brain that the emotion is too dangerous to face, which makes it feel more dangerous, which makes the next craving for distraction even stronger.

### The script you didn't write

If you're feeling exposed, the comfort food pattern doesn't mean you're broken. You were taught a script in childhood. A caring adult, lacking better tools, offered you food when you were upset: "Here. This will make you feel better." And it did, temporarily, because sugar works on a child's brain the same way it works on an adult's. The lesson was encoded: when you feel bad, eat.

The script was wrong. The cookie didn't heal the skinned knee. It provided a temporary chemical override and left the real healing postponed. Had you been taught to bang your head against a wall to soothe your emotions, you'd have grown up doing that, and wondering why you always had a headache. The comfort food association was programmed the same way. You don't need to blame yourself. You can recognize that the lesson was wrong and learn a different one.

### What emotions actually need

At this point a fear might be surfacing. It's the Big Monster's most effective defense in the comfort food arena, and it sounds like this: *"If I can't use food to cope with difficult emotions, what am I supposed to do? Just suffer? Just sit there and feel terrible with no relief?"*

This fear rests on a false premise: that your only options are "use junk food to numb" or "suffer unbearably." A third option actually works. Feel the emotion, understand what it's asking for, and address the need instead of silencing the signal.

Emotions are signals, your mind and body's way of communicating important information about your internal state and your relationship to your environment. Each one has a purpose, a message, a need it's trying to communicate.

Sadness says something that matters to you has been lost or damaged. It asks for acknowledgment, for mourning, for the slow process of integration that lets loss become part of your story instead of an open wound. When you're sad, the need is for processing, and numbing doesn't meet it. Crying, talking to someone who listens, writing, and walking while letting the feelings move through you all help. Sadness processed becomes wisdom and depth, and sadness numbed becomes depression.

Loneliness says you need connection. It's a social signal, evolved over millions of years, letting you know your need for human contact is going unmet. The appropriate response is to reach out: call a friend, go somewhere people are, join something, build the relationships that make life meaningful. Eating junk food in response to loneliness is like putting a bandage over a fuel gauge. The gauge stops bothering you, and the tank is still empty.

Boredom says your current activity isn't engaging you. It's a call to find something stimulating, such as a project, a hobby, a book, a conversation, or a challenge. Boredom is the starting gun for creativity, and when you silence it with junk food you miss the chance to discover what would actually interest you.

Anxiety says there's something you need to prepare for or confront. It's your brain running threat simulations to keep you safe. Sometimes the threat is real and the anxiety is useful, because it motivates preparation. Sometimes the threat is imagined or exaggerated, and the anxiety needs to be examined and challenged before you act on it. In either case junk food only pauses the anxiety, and it returns stronger when the chemical distraction wears off.

Using junk food to silence any of these emotions is like putting tape over the check engine light on your car's dashboard. The light stops bothering you, and for a while you can drive in peace. But the engine is still failing, and by ignoring it you're letting it get worse. Eventually the engine fails completely, and you're stranded on the side of the road wondering how this happened when the light was "off."

Your emotions are the dashboard. They tell you what you need. The problem was the industrial product sold to you as a universal emotional solution, one that addresses none of the signals and just disables your ability to hear them.

### The void that food can't fill

One category of emotional eating deserves its own look, because it is both the most common and the most resistant to the logic we've been applying.

It isn't sadness, or loneliness exactly, or boredom in the ordinary sense. It's a vague, nameless emptiness that descends at certain times: a Sunday afternoon, a quiet evening, the hour after work when the day's structure dissolves and nothing rushes in to fill the space. Nothing is specifically wrong and no crisis has occurred. But there's a hollow feeling, a sense that something is missing, a restlessness that searches for something, anything, to fill the void.

Junk food is the most available filler. It's cheap, fast, and requires no planning, effort, or vulnerability. It fills the mouth and the stomach and, briefly, the attention. It provides a small, reliable hit of sensation in an otherwise flat emotional landscape, and it works for exactly as long as the eating lasts.

Trying to fill an existential emptiness with junk food is like trying to fill a hole in the ground by throwing cotton candy into it. The cotton candy looks substantial and has volume, and for a moment, dropped into the darkness, it seems to be working. But cotton candy dissolves on contact and vanishes almost instantly, and the hole remains exactly as deep as it was. The hole needs soil and rock and substance: connection, meaning, purpose, engagement with the world. Those things don't dissolve, and they actually occupy space.

The emptiness you feel during those quiet hours is real, and it deserves attention. It might be telling you that you need more connection in your life, or more meaning in your work, or more creativity in your leisure, or more presence in your relationships. It might also mean you need to learn to be comfortable with stillness, a skill that modern life, with its constant sensory stimulation, has systematically eroded.

Junk food was never designed to fill existential needs. It was designed to create a brief sensory distraction from them. By providing a temporary, ineffective solution, it also prevents you from seeking a real one. Every time you fill the emptiness with food, you miss an opportunity to discover what would actually fill it, and every time you silence the signal, you postpone the search for what it was trying to tell you.

You've been making a category error, using a physical substance to address a non-physical need, and recognizing it lets you stop blaming yourself for the solution's failure. The job requires connection, meaning, purpose, and engagement. The tool was sugar, fat, and chemical flavorings, and no amount of skill with the wrong tool will complete the right job.

### What comfort actually feels like

The Big Monster's final argument is that without junk food, you'll have no comfort at all. That's a lie. Real comfort exists in connection, movement, rest, and creative expression, and it works better than junk food ever did.

Junk food is to genuine comfort what a cardboard cutout is to a real friend. It has the rough shape of what you need, but it can't listen, hold your hand, or sit with you in the dark. Real comfort is a living presence. The desire for comfort is one of the most human things about you, and the product sold to fulfill that desire was a counterfeit that provided brief chemical silence and no genuine peace. You deserve the genuine thing, and you always did.
